📅 This Week in Hispanic/Latino History
May 18, 1911 — The Mexican Revolution Reshapes a Nation

On this week in 1911, revolutionary leader Francisco Madero entered Mexico City as Porfirio Díaz resigned after 35 years in power — one of the most dramatic political turning points in Latin American history. The Mexican Revolution (1910–1920) was not just a political uprising; it was a profound social movement that reshaped land ownership, workers' rights, and national identity across Mexico. Its echoes are still felt today in Mexican culture, muralism, and political thought — and in the communities many of our colleagues and families trace their roots to.

📖 Deep Dive
The Women of the Mexican Revolution

When we talk about the Revolution, we often hear about Zapata and Villa — but the Soldaderas (also called Adelitas) were women who fought alongside men, served as nurses and spies, and in many cases led troops themselves. Figures like Juana Ramona de Flores ("La Tigresa") commanded guerrilla units. Their contributions were largely written out of official history for decades. The Adelita became a powerful symbol of Latina strength and resilience that still resonates in folk music and art today.
🎵 Cultural Spotlight — Music
Listen this weekend: "La Adelita" — a corrido (Mexican folk ballad) from the Revolution era. It's one of the oldest and most beloved songs in Mexican folk tradition, and it's a direct tribute to the Soldaderas. You can find numerous versions on Spotify and YouTube — try the Mariachi Vargas de Tecalitlán version for an iconic take.

¡Buenos días, HOLA familia! Hope everyone had a great weekend. This week we're heading to the southern tip of South America for a story about the birth of a nation — and a political moment that still shapes Argentina's national identity today.
📅 This Week in Hispanic/Latino History
May 25, 1810 — Argentina's May Revolution & the Primera Junta

On May 25, 1810, Buenos Aires formed the Primera Junta — the first independent governing body in what would become Argentina. It wasn't a full declaration of independence yet (that came in 1816), but it was the critical first crack in Spanish colonial authority in the Río de la Plata region. The date is now celebrated as Día de la Patria, Argentina's national day. Every year, Argentines gather in the Plaza de Mayo — the same square where it all began — to mark the moment their nation started becoming itself.

📖 Deep Dive
José Hernández & Martín Fierro — Born May 28, 1834

Today marks the birthday of José Hernández, the Argentine poet who wrote Martín Fierro (1872) — widely considered the national epic of Argentina. The poem follows a gaucho (Argentine cowboy) named Martín Fierro who is forced into military service, loses his family, and wanders the pampas in exile. It's a story about identity, injustice, and what it means to belong to a land. Hernández wrote it as a protest against the brutal conscription policies of the Argentine government — giving a voice to a people who had none. Today Martín Fierro is quoted the way Americans quote the Constitution: as a source of national character and values.
🍽️ Cultural Spotlight — Food
Try this weekend: Empanadas Argentinas

No dish is more deeply tied to Argentine identity than the empanada. Every region has its own filling — beef with hard-boiled egg and olives in the northwest, humita (corn and cheese) in Salta, spinach and ricotta in Buenos Aires. If you've never made them at home, they're a surprisingly fun weekend project. Search "empanadas salteñas recipe" for a classic starting point — and bonus, they freeze beautifully.

¡Feliz lunes, HOLA familia! Welcome to June — and to a story that connects Latin American history to one of the greatest environmental breakthroughs of the 20th century.
📅 This Week in Hispanic/Latino History
June 1995 — Mario Molina Accepts the Nobel Prize in Chemistry

In 1995, Mexican-born scientist Mario Molina became the first Mexican citizen to win the Nobel Prize in Chemistry. His research, conducted alongside F. Sherwood Rowland, proved that chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs) were destroying the ozone layer — work that directly led to the Montreal Protocol, the most successful international environmental treaty in history. Molina grew up in Mexico City, trained in Germany and the United States, and spent his career proving that rigorous science could change global policy. He passed away in 2020, but his legacy protects every person on Earth.

👋 Opener
¡Hola, familia! The weekend is close. Let's close out this week's theme with a deeper look and a great cultural recommendation.
📖 Deep Dive
Ellen Ochoa — First Latina in Space

In April 1993, Ellen Ochoa became the first Hispanic woman to travel to space aboard the Space Shuttle Discovery. Born in Los Angeles to a Mexican-American family, she holds three patents in optical systems and went on to become Director of NASA's Johnson Space Center. Ochoa has said she was inspired by reading about people who pursued their passions — and that she didn't originally plan on becoming an astronaut, but let curiosity lead her. She represents an extraordinary through-line of Latino scientific excellence.
📚 Cultural Spotlight — Book
Read this month: The Alchemist by Paulo Coelho (Brazilian author, translated from Portuguese). A short, powerful novel about following your purpose across the world. It has sold over 65 million copies and remains one of the most widely read books ever written by a Latin American author. Perfect for a long weekend afternoon.
