📅 This Week in Hispanic/Latino History
May 18, 1911 — The Mexican Revolution Reshapes a Nation

On this week in 1911, revolutionary leader Francisco Madero entered Mexico City as Porfirio Díaz resigned after 35 years in power — one of the most dramatic political turning points in Latin American history. The Mexican Revolution (1910–1920) was not just a political uprising; it was a profound social movement that reshaped land ownership, workers' rights, and national identity across Mexico. Its echoes are still felt today in Mexican culture, muralism, and political thought — and in the communities many of our colleagues and families trace their roots to.

📖 Deep Dive
The Women of the Mexican Revolution

When we talk about the Revolution, we often hear about Zapata and Villa — but the Soldaderas (also called Adelitas) were women who fought alongside men, served as nurses and spies, and in many cases led troops themselves. Figures like Juana Ramona de Flores ("La Tigresa") commanded guerrilla units. Their contributions were largely written out of official history for decades. The Adelita became a powerful symbol of Latina strength and resilience that still resonates in folk music and art today.
🎵 Cultural Spotlight — Music
Listen this weekend: "La Adelita" — a corrido (Mexican folk ballad) from the Revolution era. It's one of the oldest and most beloved songs in Mexican folk tradition, and it's a direct tribute to the Soldaderas. You can find numerous versions on Spotify and YouTube — try the Mariachi Vargas de Tecalitlán version for an iconic take.

May 25, 1810 — Argentina's May Revolution & the Primera Junta

On May 25, 1810, Buenos Aires formed the Primera Junta — the first independent governing body in what would become Argentina. It wasn't a full declaration of independence yet (that came in 1816), but it was the critical first crack in Spanish colonial authority in the Río de la Plata region. The date is now celebrated as Día de la Patria, Argentina's national day. Every year, Argentines gather in the Plaza de Mayo — the same square where it all began — to mark the moment their nation started becoming itself.

📖 Deep Dive
José Hernández & Martín Fierro — Born May 28, 1834

Today marks the birthday of José Hernández, the Argentine poet who wrote Martín Fierro (1872) — widely considered the national epic of Argentina. The poem follows a gaucho (Argentine cowboy) named Martín Fierro who is forced into military service, loses his family, and wanders the pampas in exile. It's a story about identity, injustice, and what it means to belong to a land. Hernández wrote it as a protest against the brutal conscription policies of the Argentine government — giving a voice to a people who had none. Today Martín Fierro is quoted the way Americans quote the Constitution: as a source of national character and values.
🍽️ Cultural Spotlight — Food
Try this weekend: Empanadas Argentinas

No dish is more deeply tied to Argentine identity than the empanada. Every region has its own filling — beef with hard-boiled egg and olives in the northwest, humita (corn and cheese) in Salta, spinach and ricotta in Buenos Aires. If you've never made them at home, they're a surprisingly fun weekend project. Search "empanadas salteñas recipe" for a classic starting point — and bonus, they freeze beautifully.

📅 This Week in Hispanic/Latino History
June 1995 — Mario Molina Accepts the Nobel Prize in Chemistry

In 1995, Mexican-born scientist Mario Molina became the first Mexican citizen to win the Nobel Prize in Chemistry. His research, conducted alongside F. Sherwood Rowland, proved that chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs) were destroying the ozone layer — work that directly led to the Montreal Protocol, the most successful international environmental treaty in history. Molina grew up in Mexico City, trained in Germany and the United States, and spent his career proving that rigorous science could change global policy. He passed away in 2020, but his legacy protects every person on Earth.

Ellen Ochoa — First Latina in Space

In April 1993, Ellen Ochoa became the first Hispanic woman to travel to space aboard the Space Shuttle Discovery. Born in Los Angeles to a Mexican-American family, she holds three patents in optical systems and went on to become Director of NASA's Johnson Space Center. Ochoa has said she was inspired by reading about people who pursued their passions — and that she didn't originally plan on becoming an astronaut, but let curiosity lead her. She represents an extraordinary through-line of Latino scientific excellence.

June 8, 1889 — Birthday of Gabriela Mistral

Born Lucila Godoy Alcayaga in Chile's Elqui Valley, Gabriela Mistral spent years as a rural schoolteacher before the world discovered her as a poet. In 1945 she became the first Latin American and first woman from the Americas to win the Nobel Prize in Literature. She dedicated her Nobel Prize to the children of Latin America.

¡Hola, familia! This Thursday we go deeper on Puerto Rico's complicated relationship with the United States — a story of identity, sovereignty, and unbreakable resilience.
📖 Deep Dive
The Foraker Act (1900) — Puerto Rico's Uneasy Status

When the U.S. acquired Puerto Rico from Spain after the Spanish-American War in 1898, Puerto Ricans entered a legal limbo that persists today. The Foraker Act of 1900 made Puerto Rico a U.S. territory without granting citizenship. The Jones Act of 1917 eventually granted citizenship — just in time to draft Puerto Ricans into WWI. The questions of statehood, independence, or commonwealth status remain central to Puerto Rican political life and identity. Whatever one's view, Puerto Rico's story is inseparable from the broader story of Latino identity in the United States.
🍽️ Cultural Spotlight — Food
Cook this weekend: Mofongo — Puerto Rico's most beloved dish. Fried green plantains mashed with garlic, salt, and chicharrón, shaped into a mound and filled with shrimp, chicken, or vegetables in savory broth. Search "mofongo de camarones" for a classic version.

Celia Cruz — Born June 18, 1925

Born in Havana, Celia Cruz rose to fame with the Sonora Matancera before going into exile when Castro came to power in 1959 — she never returned, even to bury her parents. 📖 Deep Dive — Día de San Juan
Walking Backwards into the Ocean

Día de San Juan Bautista (June 24) is Puerto Rico's patron saint feast day and one of its most joyful summer celebrations. The island fills with music and the famous midnight tradition: walking backwards into the ocean seven times to receive good fortune. The custom merges pre-Christian water purification rituals with Catholic feast-day observance. In Puerto Rico, this is one of the biggest parties of the year — celebrated on every beach from Luquillo to Rincón.
🏆 Also This Week — Roberto Clemente
Roberto Clemente's 3,000th hit (September 30, 1972) is honored this week. It was his last. Three months later he died delivering aid to earthquake victims in Nicaragua. His number 21 was retired across all of Major League Baseball — the only player besides Jackie Robinson to receive that honor.
🎵 Cultural Spotlight — Music
Listen this weekend: La Borinqueña — Puerto Rico's anthem, written by Lola Rodríguez de Tió in 1868 as a revolutionary call to arms. Then put on some bomba y plena — Puerto Rico's African-rooted percussion tradition, the heartbeat of island culture.

📖 Deep Dive
July 9, 1816 — Argentina's Independence Day

Argentina's independence was declared in Tucumán — deliberately not Buenos Aires — as a symbolic act of defiance against royalist influence in the capital. The declaration was read aloud in Spanish, Quechua, and Aymara, acknowledging the indigenous nations whose lands and labor formed the country. Manuel Belgrano designed the celeste-and-white flag after the color of the sky. July 9 is celebrated nationwide with asados, folk music, and that distinctive Argentinian flag waving from every window.

July 6, 1907 — Birthday of Frida Kahlo

Born in Coyoacán, Mexico City, Frida Kahlo was nearly killed at 18 in a bus accident that shattered her spine, collarbone, and pelvis. She spent her recovery teaching herself to paint in bed using a specially built easel and a mirror mounted above her. Over her lifetime she created 143 paintings — most self-portraits — that became some of the most powerful explorations of identity, gender, and pain ever put on canvas. Frida once said she painted her own reality because it was the one she knew best.

📖 Deep Dive
Federico García Lorca — Born July 5, 1898

Born in Granada, Andalusia, García Lorca became the most beloved Spanish poet of the 20th century. His concept of duende — the mysterious creative force that gives art its power — remains one of the most important ideas in Spanish artistic culture. In 1936 he was arrested by Nationalist forces and executed at 38. His body was never found. His murder became a symbol of what fascism does to beauty and truth, and his work has never stopped being read.

August 3, 1492 — Columbus Departs Palos de la Frontera

On August 3, 1492, Columbus departed Spain with three ships and sailed into the unknown, arriving in the Bahamas on October 12. The legacy of 1492 is deeply complex: it brought catastrophic disease, displacement, and enslavement to indigenous peoples, and simultaneously began the cultural fusion that created the Latino world as we know it. Latino identity is inseparable from this history — not as victims or inheritors, but as a people who survived, adapted, resisted, and ultimately created something entirely new.

Before Cortés arrived, Tenochtitlán was one of the largest cities in the world — built on an island in a lake, with aqueducts, floating gardens, and a population of perhaps 200,000 people at a time when London had fewer than 100,000. Hispanic Heritage Month runs September 15 through October 15. The African Roots of Latin America

Over 90% of the approximately 12 million enslaved Africans transported to the Americas went to Latin America and the Caribbean, not North America. Their story is essential, not supplemental, to the Latino story.

📖 Deep Dive — How to Build an Ofrenda
The Ofrenda — A Guide

An ofrenda is built in layers — typically two or three tiers representing Earth and heaven. What goes on it: Cempasúchil (marigolds) — their scent guides spirits home. Copal incense — purifies the air. Photos of the departed — they anchor the altar. Their favorite foods and drinks — offered so they can enjoy them one more time. A glass of water — to quench their thirst after the journey. Candles — to light the way. Pan de muerto — the sweet bread baked for this day. You do not need to be Mexican or Catholic to build one. In 1531, a recently converted indigenous man named Juan Diego reported an apparition of the Virgin Mary on the hill of Tepeyac, just north of Mexico City. The image she left imprinted on his tilma (cloak) — a woman with indigenous features, surrounded by light — became one of the most reproduced images in human history. The Virgen de Guadalupe is more than a religious icon; she is a symbol of mestizo identity, of the fusion of indigenous and Spanish cultures, and of protection for the poor. Millions travel to the Basilica of Guadalupe in Mexico City every December 12, making it one of the most visited Catholic shrines in the world.

Las Posadas — Nine Nights of Song and Community

Las Posadas (December 16–24) reenact the journey of Mary and Joseph seeking shelter in Bethlehem — posada means "inn" or "lodging" in Spanish. Each night, a procession moves through the neighborhood carrying candles and figures of Mary and Joseph. Participants sing a traditional call-and-response song: pilgrims outside begging for shelter, hosts inside refusing — until finally the doors are thrown open in joyful welcome. The tradition was actually introduced by Spanish missionaries in the 1500s as a teaching tool, but indigenous communities adopted and transformed it into something uniquely Mexican. Today it is celebrated across Mexico, Central America, and U.S. Latino communities with piñatas, ponche (hot fruit punch), tamales, and buñuelos.

📖 Deep Dive
Why Christmas Eve is the Real Celebration

In most of Latin America and Latino households in the United States, December 24 — Nochebuena — is the main event. The feast happens at night, families stay up past midnight, and gifts are often exchanged at 12:01 AM. December 25 is quieter — a day for leftovers, family visits, and rest. The tradition traces back to Catholic liturgical practice (midnight Mass marking the start of Christmas) merged with indigenous and African cultural rhythms of nighttime celebration. Nochebuena looks different in every country: Cuba has lechón asado and black beans; Puerto Rico has pernil and arroz con gandules; Mexico has tamales and ponche; Colombia has natilla and buñuelos. What they all share is the same spirit — la familia reunida.

Latino New Year's Eve Traditions — Superstitions, Rituals & Joy

New Year's Eve across Latin America is one of the richest nights of the year for cultural tradition.
